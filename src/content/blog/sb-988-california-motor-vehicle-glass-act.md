**Last updated October 6, 2026.** Gov. Newsom signed SB 988 on
September 27, 2026. It is now law, filed as Chapter 666, Statutes of 2026, and
takes effect January 1, 2027. You can read the chaptered text on the
[bill's page on leginfo.legislature.ca.gov](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billNavClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260SB988).
This post is a plain-English summary, not legal advice.

## Where the bill stands

SB 988, the California Motor Vehicle Glass Act, was concurred in by the Senate
on August 26, 2026 (37 to 0), enrolled and presented to the Governor on
August 31, and approved on September 27, 2026.

It adds a new Title 1.5C, sections 1784.50 through 1784.58, to the Civil Code.
The act takes effect January 1, 2027. Until then, none of it can be enforced,
so the practices below are what to ask for, not yet what a shop is required
to do.

## What the law requires of glass shops

The core of the act is a set of duties on the business doing the glass work. A
shop must:

- **Tell you whether the vehicle has ADAS** — the camera and sensor systems
  behind the windshield — before service. If it does, the shop must say
  whether the manufacturer recommends a calibration afterwards and whether the
  shop intends to do it to the manufacturer's specifications. If it can't or
  won't, it must tell you to take the vehicle to the manufacturer's certified
  dealership or a qualified specialist.
- **Give written notice of whether a calibration succeeded.** If it didn't,
  the shop must tell you not to rely on the ADAS until a dealer or qualified
  specialist has calibrated it.
- **Provide a good-faith estimate before the work**, and an updated estimate
  before performing the service.
- **Issue an itemized invoice and, on payment, a receipt** when the job is
  complete.
- **Have a claim or referral number before contracting** for work that will be
  paid by a first-party insurance policy.
- **Not charge more than reasonable and customary fees and costs**, including
  for any manufacturer-recommended calibration.

Most of that is what a careful shop already does. The act makes it a
requirement and gives a customer something to point to when it isn't done.

## What the law prohibits

The second half is a list of practices a shop may not engage in. A shop, or
anyone paid to solicit insurance claims, may not:

- Offer a rebate, gift, gift card, cash, coupon, prize, bonus or any other
  thing of value in exchange for directing or making a glass claim.

A shop also may not:

- Charge more than a reasonable price for the area.
- Submit false, misleading or incomplete documentation or information to a
  customer or an insurer.
- Misrepresent the price of the work to a customer or an insurer.
- Say an insurer has approved the work without verifying coverage with the
  insurer or its agent and getting that confirmation in writing or on a
  recorded channel.
- Say the work will be paid for entirely by the insurer at no cost to you
  unless the insurer or its agent has verified the coverage.
- Damage a vehicle, or encourage you to, to increase the scope of the repair.
- Falsely sign a work order or insurance form, or advise you to falsify the
  date of damage.
- Report work as done in a different geographic area from where it happened,
  when that would raise the insurance payment or change coverage status.
- Perform work clearly beyond what is needed to restore the vehicle to a safe
  pre-damage condition.
- Misrepresent its relationship to you or the insurer's agent.

Several of those describe specific patterns that have shown up in glass
claims — the "free windshield" pitch that turns out to be a claim against the
customer's policy, and the incentive-for-referral arrangement — which is
presumably why they're named.

## What it means for your insurance claim

Two provisions are aimed at the relationship between you, the shop and your
insurer:

- **You can't assign your policy benefits.** An insured under a policy that
  covers windshield and calibration work may not assign, delegate or transfer
  their duties, rights or benefits under the policy, and a contract that does
  so, entered into on or after January 1, 2027, is void and unenforceable. The
  act does not stop you from authorizing or directing payment to a shop for
  work the policy covers.
- **You keep the right to choose your shop.** You can't be required to use a
  particular shop or recalibration facility to get claim payments. Independent
  and dealership, network and non-network — your choice. An insurer can still
  recommend a shop, explain your coverage and keep a network, but it may not
  purposefully mislead, threaten or coerce you about which shop to use.
  (California already says something similar in Insurance Code § 758.5 — see
  [does insurance cover windshield replacement](/blog/windshield-insurance-claim/)
  — so this reinforces existing law rather than creating a new right.)

## Enforcement

Violations carry a civil penalty of up to $500 for the first and up to $2,000
for each later one, recovered in a civil action brought by a city attorney,
county counsel or the Attorney General. Those are the only dollar amounts in
the act.

## What isn't in it

A few things people might expect from a "glass act" that it doesn't do:

- It doesn't set a price list or fee schedule for glass work. It limits
  charges to reasonable and customary fees, which is a standard, not a number.
- It doesn't require insurers to cover glass, waive deductibles, or offer any
  particular coverage. Whether a windshield is covered stays a policy question.
- It doesn't license auto glass technicians or create a certification.
- It doesn't tell you whether your chip is a repair or a replacement — that's
  still the damage itself. The
  [repair or replace guide](/blog/repair-or-replace-windshield-damage/)
  covers that.

## Why the ADAS disclosure matters most

Of everything in the act, the calibration disclosure is the part most likely
to change what a customer experiences. On a camera-equipped vehicle, a
[windshield replacement](/windshield-replacement/) moves the camera, and the
manufacturer typically specifies a recalibration afterwards. Today, whether
you're told that — and whether you get anything in writing about it — depends
on the shop. From January 1, 2027 you should be told up front, and you should
get a written result.

A resin fill on a chip doesn't involve the camera, so [fixing a chip early](/windshield-chip-repair/)
keeps you out of the calibration question altogether.
