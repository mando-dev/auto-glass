Short answer: it can be, and the test isn't the size of the crack. California
law is about whether the damage gets in the way of you seeing the road.

## What the law actually says

The section that matters is Vehicle Code § 26710. Stripped to its two working
parts, it says:

1. You can't drive a vehicle on the highway when "the windshield or rear window
   is in such a defective condition as to impair the driver's vision either to
   the front or rear."
2. If an officer stops you for it, they may direct you to "make the windshield
   and rear window conform to the requirements of this code within 48 hours."

That's the whole standard. You can read it on the Legislature's site:
[Vehicle Code § 26710](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=VEH&sectionNum=26710).

Two things worth noticing about the wording.

**There's no measurement in it.** People often repeat a rule like "anything
bigger than a dollar bill" or "any crack over six inches." Those are useful as
repair-versus-replace guidance, but they don't come from § 26710. The statute
never mentions inches, dollar bills or quarters. It asks one question: does
the condition of the glass impair the driver's vision?

**It covers the rear window too.** The section applies to the windshield and
the rear window equally, so a badly damaged back glass can put you in the
same position as a cracked front one.

## What "impairs the driver's vision" means in practice

Because the statute is written as a judgement rather than a measurement, the
same crack can be a problem in one place on the glass and not in another.

- A long crack running across the driver's side at eye level is the clearest
  case. It sits in the line of sight, it catches light, and at night it
  scatters oncoming headlights. That is what the statute is describing.
- A chip low on the passenger side, or a short crack up near the mirror
  mount, is far less likely to be read as impairing vision.
- Damage that's spreading is a moving target. A crack that starts at the edge
  and runs toward the middle of the glass will cross into the driver's view
  eventually, and California heat tends to speed that up — see
  [why chips spread faster in the heat](/blog/windshield-damage-orange-county-heat/).

The safest way to think about it: if you find yourself looking around the
damage rather than through the glass, you're in the territory the section is
about.

## The 48-hour part

Section 26710 gives an officer the option to direct the driver to bring the
glass into compliance within 48 hours. That's a correction window, and it's
short on purpose — two days is enough to get the glass fixed, not enough to
put it off for a month.

If you're given that direction, the practical answer is to get the repair or
replacement done inside the window and keep the paperwork. Whether a small
chip can be resin-repaired or the glass needs replacing depends on the
damage itself; the [repair or replace guide](/blog/repair-or-replace-windshield-damage/)
walks through the four things that decide it.

## What this doesn't tell you

A few things people ask that § 26710 doesn't answer:

- **It doesn't set a fine amount.** Penalties for Vehicle Code violations are
  handled elsewhere and can vary; this article isn't the place to guess at a
  number.
- **It doesn't say anything about insurance.** Whether a claim covers the
  repair is a policy question, not a Vehicle Code one — see
  [does insurance cover windshield replacement in California](/blog/windshield-insurance-claim/).
- **It doesn't distinguish repair from replacement.** The statute cares about
  the outcome — glass that doesn't impair vision — not how you get there.
- **It doesn't cover tint, stickers or cargo.** Other sections of the Vehicle
  Code deal with what you're allowed to attach to or hang from the glass. This
  one is about the condition of the glass itself, so a crack and an
  obstruction are separate questions even though both end in the same place:
  can the driver see?

One more practical point: a windshield that passes on a clear afternoon can
fail at night, when a crack across the driver's side turns every set of
oncoming headlights into a streak. If you only ever look at the damage in
daylight, it's worth checking it once after dark before deciding it's fine.

## What to do if you're driving on a crack right now

If the crack is in the driver's line of sight, or it's long enough that it's
heading there, treat it as a replacement job and don't wait on it. On a
camera-equipped vehicle, replacing the glass also means asking about ADAS
recalibration afterwards — the [replacement page](/windshield-replacement/)
