If your car has lane-keeping, automatic emergency braking or adaptive cruise
control, there is a camera mounted to the windshield behind the mirror doing
a lot of that work. Replace the glass and that camera gets moved. This post
explains why that matters, what recalibration is, and what to ask when you
book a replacement.

## What the camera behind the mirror does

Most vehicles built from roughly 2018 onward carry a forward-facing camera
mounted to the inside of the windshield. It reads lane markings, watches for
vehicles and pedestrians ahead, and feeds that to the driver-assistance
systems — collectively called ADAS, for advanced driver assistance systems.
On some vehicles it's the main sensor for those features; on others it works
alongside radar.

The camera's usefulness depends on it pointing exactly where the manufacturer
expects. It's aimed to a fraction of a degree relative to the vehicle's
centerline, and the software assumes that aim is correct.

## Why replacing the glass changes that

The camera is fixed to a bracket bonded to the windshield. When the
windshield comes out, the camera comes with it. When new glass goes in, the
camera is re-mounted to the new bracket — and even a very careful
installation can leave it a fraction off from where it was.

A fraction of a degree at the camera becomes a meaningful distance a hundred
yards down the road. That's why manufacturers typically specify a
recalibration after a windshield replacement on camera-equipped vehicles:
not because anything went wrong, but because the camera's reference point has
moved and the system needs to be told where it is now.

## Static and dynamic calibration

There are two methods, and which one a vehicle needs is set by the
manufacturer, not by the shop.

**Static calibration** happens with the vehicle parked. A target board is set
up at a precise distance and height in front of the car, on level ground with
controlled lighting, and a diagnostic tool walks the camera through
recognising the target. It needs space and a controlled environment.

**Dynamic calibration** happens on the road. A diagnostic tool is connected and
the vehicle is driven at a set speed on well-marked roads for a set distance
while the camera relearns its reference from real lane lines. It needs the
right kind of road and the right conditions.

**Some vehicles need both.** Static first, then a dynamic drive to confirm.

Neither is a five-minute add-on. Both depend on the procedure being done to
the manufacturer's specification, and both should end with a scan showing the
system has accepted the calibration.

## A chip repair doesn't trigger this

Worth stating plainly: a resin chip repair does not require recalibration. The
repair fills the break in the outer layer of the glass. The windshield stays
in the vehicle, the bracket stays bonded, the camera stays where it was. The
[chip repair](/windshield-chip-repair/) page covers what a repair does and
doesn't do.

That's one of the practical reasons to deal with a chip early: while it's a
chip, it's a repair. Once it's a crack, it's a replacement, and on a
camera-equipped vehicle a replacement brings the calibration question with it.

## What to ask whoever does the replacement

Regardless of who you book with, three questions cover it:

1. **Does my vehicle need recalibration after this?** The answer should be
   specific to your year, make, model and trim — the same model can carry
   different sensor packages.
2. **Is it included, and who does it?** Some shops calibrate in-house, some
   sublet it to a specialist, some don't handle it at all. There's nothing
   wrong with any of those as long as you know which one it is before the
   glass is out.
3. **Is it documented?** Ask for the pre-scan and post-scan reports, or the
   calibration confirmation, in writing. If the system's status isn't
   recorded, you have no way of knowing whether it was done or whether it
   succeeded.

If the answer to the first question is "we're not sure," that's a reason to
pause and find out before booking rather than after.
